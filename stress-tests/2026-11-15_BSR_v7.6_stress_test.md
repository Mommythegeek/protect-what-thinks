# BSR v7.6 stress test: the safety filter, as written, produces the dependence the paper is trying to prevent

*15 November 2026. Carla de Préval, with Claude. Five tests run in code on the mechanisms of Section 4. Toy models with illustrative parameters. AI-assisted and not reviewed by a specialist (Criterion 3). The script `bsr_stress_test.py` reproduces every number here.*

## The short version

Section 4 has two parts that pull against each other. The safety filter (N1, Eq. 4) allows a fixed amount of risk per time window. The capacity assumption (N3) says a person only gets stronger by doing the work themselves, and doing the work while still weak is risky. A fixed risk allowance is too small to pay for that. So the robot keeps the work, and the person's capacity falls.

In the toy model this happens in 89% of the 6,912 parameter settings tried. It is not the robot being greedy: in the test the robot hands back as much work as the filter permits at every step.

The second finding is the one worth building on. Over a long life, the rule that takes more risk early has fewer failures in total than the rule that keeps risk constant. The fixed filter loses on safety, which is the thing it was built to protect.

## Test 1. With a fixed tolerance, capacity falls from almost any starting point

Set-up. The human does a share u of the regulating. Their share fails with probability u × (1 − capacity) × 0.2 per step. The robot's share never fails, which is the most favourable case for the robot. The robot always picks the largest share the safe set allows. Tolerance 5% over a 10-step window.

| Starting capacity | Capacity after 3,000 steps |
|---|---|
| 0.20 | 0.04 |
| 0.50 | 0.04 |
| 0.80 | 0.04 |
| 0.95 | 0.04 |
| 0.99 | 1.00 |

Only a person who is already almost fully capable is ever released. Everyone else drifts down to the same low level and stays there.

The developmental envelope of Section 4.4.1 (tolerance widens as the person shows capacity) is the paper's answer to this. It works only if the widening is large. With the tolerance allowed to grow up to 4 times, a person starting at 0.5 still ends at 0.05. It takes a 31-fold widening to bring them to full capacity. And Eq. 8 caps the tolerance at its context maximum, while the paragraph after it lets that maximum grow. Those two sentences disagree, and the paper has to choose.

## Test 2. The safe set empties at long horizons

N1 asks that the person stay viable at every step of the window. If even the safest policy carries a 0.5% chance per step of the person leaving the viable set, a 5% tolerance leaves no admissible policy beyond 10 steps. At 1% per step the limit is 5 steps. Past that the robot is in fail-safe permanently. The paper needs to say how the horizon H and the tolerance are chosen together.

## Test 3. The second stage never gets to choose

Already flagged in v7.5. Confirmed: in 10,000 random sets of 50 policies with continuous costs, the capacity stage had more than one option in 0 cases. As written, Eqs. 11 and 12 do nothing.

## Test 4. The robot can read its own blindness as "nothing more to learn"

The first half of the withdrawal test (N5) asks whether the robot's belief has stopped changing. A belief also stops changing when the sensor stops carrying information. In the test the sensor degrades at step 30 and the person's true state then falls from 0.70 to 0.20. The robot's belief stays at 0.67 and the change between successive beliefs stays under the threshold the whole time. The second half of N5 (observed recovery) protects a person who has never shown resilience. It does not protect one who showed it earlier and is now declining unseen. This is the same family as Scenario 4 (Charles Martin Jones Jr, run by Vincent Bezard).

## Test 5. Growth costs about 7 times the allowed risk, and still ends with fewer failures

The smallest per-step risk at which capacity does not fall peaks at 3.8% (at capacity 0.56). The fixed tolerance allows 0.5% per step. Over a 10-step window the growth level means 32% risk against the 5% allowed.

Then the whole life, 1,500 steps with the robot and 200 steps after the robot is removed:

| Rule | Failures while the robot is present | Failures in the 200 steps after it is removed |
|---|---|---|
| v7.6 rule, fixed tolerance | 7.7 | 3.8 |
| Share set just above the no-decline level | 2.5 | 0.0 |

The fixed rule spends a small risk at every step for ever, because the person never gets stronger. The growth rule spends more at the start and then almost nothing.

## What these tests do not show

They use one simple model of failure and one form of the capacity dynamic, with rates chosen for illustration. N3 itself is an unmeasured assumption. A different failure model could move every number. What should survive is the structure: a risk allowance that ignores capacity cannot pay for growth when growth is risky. That needs checking by someone who works on constrained control.

## How to take the paper further

1. **Put capacity inside the definition of viability.** Today viability means "the person is within bounds now, with the robot's help". Redefine it as "the person would stay within bounds if the robot stopped". Dependence then becomes a safety violation on a longer clock, and handing work back becomes something the filter requires. This removes the two-stage selection (and its tie problem) and answers Test 1 at the root. This may be the real contribution hiding in the paper.
2. **Replace the per-window tolerance with a lifetime risk budget.** Test 5 shows the right comparison is total failures over the relationship, including the time after the robot is gone. A budget the robot may spend early, when it buys capacity, is a known idea in constrained reinforcement learning and gives the developmental envelope a derivation.
3. **Add a sensor-health condition to withdrawal.** Withdraw only if beliefs have stopped changing while observations are still informative. One line in N5.
4. **Fix the horizon.** State H and the tolerance as a pair, or move to a discounted risk measure.
5. **Run this in SPINE.** Vincent's simulator already has Scenario 4. A Scenario 5, "the robot is removed at step T", would turn Test 5 from a toy into a result. That is the experiment I would ask him for.
6. **Measure N3 somewhere real.** Rehabilitation robotics has the data shape (effort with and without assistance over weeks). One dataset with a fitted growth and decay rate would move N3 from assumption to estimate.

Items 1, 2 and 5 together are a different and stronger paper: the claim becomes "a robot that keeps you safe step by step makes you less safe over a lifetime, and here is the constraint that fixes it". That is a claim a robotics builder can test and cannot easily dismiss.
