Specification

The mechanism in plain words
  • Capability in a domain follows an S-curve towards a ceiling below 100%. Physical work and slow feedback lower the ceiling.
  • Plans run ahead of capability. Firms plan automation on the capability they expect λ·anticipation years ahead, and cut junior hiring on those plans. Actual automation cannot exceed what machines can do, and happens only where machines are cheaper. The gap between plans and reality is the cost of over-anticipation.
  • The self-fulfilling loop runs through people, not machines. Fewer juniors drain the stock of experienced judgment, which makes the human review look less necessary. Machine capability itself does not depend on it.
  • Demand and new tasks. Cheaper output raises demand, and capability creates new tasks (orchestration, evaluation, agent management). Both partly refill human work. People who lose their jobs also stop spending, unless gains are shared.
  • Rivalry pushes automation past the shared optimum. A firm keeps the whole wage saving when it automates but feels only its own share of the lost spending, so many rivals together automate more than any of them would choose jointly (Hemenway Falk and Tsoukalas, 2026). Sharing gains softens the lost spending but, as in their paper, does not remove the pull; only a corrective tax does. The tool reports the extra automation.
  • Which tasks go first matters. When machines take the routine, junior tasks first, entry hiring and practice fall hardest; when they take expert tasks first, juniors are spared and experts lose practice (Autor and Thompson, 2025).
  • Checking stops when output looks expert. As machine output comes to resemble skilled work, reviewers lose the ability to tell good from bad, while what is at stake stays the same; checking is done only when that ability times the stakes exceeds its cost (Cao, 2026). Opacity raises the cost. Review continues in form while inspection fades. A duty of human oversight (the liability dial) keeps some checking going regardless. This rule stands in for deference, hierarchy and the normalisation of deviance, which it does not explain.
  • The dashboard learns. When little checking is visible, review is counted more and more as cost (ε). This is the essay's measurement loop: a system that cannot see human necessity stops paying for it.
  • A predicted override rate. The tool reports how often review should change an outcome: share checked × machine error rate × judgment. It is uncalibrated, and it is one visible trace of review, not its value: a review that deters, teaches a junior or makes someone answerable leaves no trace in it. Singapore's IMDA asks firms to audit this rate.
  • Decision capacity (our index for what Aghion and Tirole call real authority) needs experienced judgment, the time to use it, and access to the evidence. Counting review as cost removes time; opaque agents remove evidence.
  • Formal sign-off authority is held up by liability. The signature-decision gap is formal minus real. The moral crumple zone (Elish, 2019) is what happens after a failure, when blame lands on the signature.
  • Feedback. Over-anticipation slowly corrects belief. A serious failure, when rare-event exposure crosses a threshold, triggers imposed friction: liability and apprenticeship rise, cost framing falls.

Prediction register, open
  1. By 31 Dec 2027, at least one listed company publicly reverses an AI-attributed headcount cut by rehiring for the same function. Mechanism: over-anticipation.
  2. In the next Stanford “Canaries” update, employment of 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations stays below its late-2022 counterfactual. Mechanism: pipeline.
  3. By 31 Dec 2028, a peer-reviewed study reports reduced unaided performance after routine AI use in a clinical specialty other than endoscopy. Mechanism: skill decay.
  4. By 31 Dec 2028, an EU or US regulator or major insurer requires documented human review or manual practice for a named clinical AI use. Mechanism: imposed friction.
  5. The next Anthropic workforce report, if published, shows meta-work above 22% of sessions and agent hours above 6 to 1. Mechanism: new tasks and cost framing.
  6. By 31 Dec 2028, a firm or regulator publishes a human override rate for an AI system in production use, and it is below 5% of reviewed outputs. Mechanism: checking stops when output looks expert.
  7. By 31 Dec 2027, a major agent-platform vendor ships a metric that reports human approval time as latency to reduce. Mechanism: cost framing.
Known limits
  • Coefficient ranges are reasoned, not fitted. The plausibility checks are in-sample.
  • Wages and prices enter only through a cost-advantage dial, a demand dial and the spending lost by people laid off, not a general equilibrium. For the macroeconomy to 2030, see Korinek, Jones and colleagues (2026) below.
  • Human necessity is modelled only as judgment that catches errors. Accountability, legitimacy and the right of the people affected to address a person (Arendt) are not in it; “human presence as a premium” treats presence as something sold.
  • The signature-decision region depends on how formal sign-off is defined. Formal sign-off rises with liability, so profiles with more liability (the EU) land there more often by construction. Holding formal sign-off equal across regions, the EU lands there less often than the US. Read decision capacity by region first.
  • Skill erosion is yearly. The endoscopy study suggests much faster short-run losses than the model allows; whether they last is unknown.
  • Human labour moved out of sight is missing: annotators, raters, moderators and offshore reviewers who make automation work (Casilli; Gray and Suri). "Work delivered by people" means people in this domain only.
  • No population layer. Agents coordinating with one another (as on Moltbook in January 2026) are not modelled, so "gradual disempowerment" is barely supported.
  • Workers appear only through apprenticeship, shared gains and voice. Resistance, workarounds and gradual drift (Vaughan's normalisation of deviance) are not modelled.
  • Regional profiles (United States, European Union, France, China) are stylised dial shifts with a named basis, not estimates. Other regions, including India and the Global South, where AI may close the services-export ladder, are not yet profiled.
  • Who bears the cost (by gender, class and country) is not modelled. Care and teaching, where the relationship shield is strongest, are mostly underpaid and done by women.
  • Apprenticeship is modelled as hiring plus how juniors use AI. Tacit and embodied skill, a sequence of real tasks that grow more central (Lave and Wenger), and the master's teaching time are not separate: dashboards that cut review time probably cut teaching time too, and the model does not link them yet.
  • The junior hiring credit has no fiscal cost and no windfall hires, and its strength is a guess. The two rows of the Stanford check are one test at two dates.
  • Taste is not measured. Signal capture stands in for it, which ignores that taste is social (Bourdieu).
  • Built with Claude, an Anthropic model, partly on Anthropic's own published data.
